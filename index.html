Trump Targets Canadian Jetmaker as Trade War Escalates

Canada's $20 billion in retaliatory tariffs took effect yesterday, and President Trump responded by calling for a ban on sales from aerospace giant Bombardier — the latest escalation in a trade fight that collapsed weeks after a preliminary deal. Morning Brew

The retaliation. Canada officially instituted its $20 billion retaliatory tariffs yesterday on steel, cosmetics, hockey sticks, appliances, and other US products, in response to existing US tariffs on the country. The Trump administration answered with even more tariffs on Canada and moved to ban imports of rye whiskies and motorcycles.

The Bombardier flashpoint. President Trump also called for a ban on sales of planes from Canadian aerospace company Bombardier, without detailing how he'd enforce it. Bombardier was quick to note its US footprint: its supply chain sources parts like wings and engines from roughly 2,800 US companies in 47 states. Kansas Sen. Jerry Moran objected, noting Bombardier supports a 1,000+ workforce in his home state.

How it fell apart. A preliminary deal on Aug. 18 looked promising, then collapsed amid dueling accusations. Canadian officials said the US wanted changes to French-language content rules on streaming platforms, which the US denied; Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick blamed an 11th-hour Canadian ask for truck tariff relief. Canadian PM Mark Carney said the retaliatory tariffs were meant to protect Canadian workers, accusing Trump of wanting Canada dependent on the US rather than a "true economic partnership."

What's next. The countries could ratchet tariffs even higher ahead of an intense US midterm season — Ontario Premier Doug Ford even floated restricting electricity exports to border states like New York and Michigan. Meanwhile, as Canada's relationship with the US sours, it's strengthening ties to Europe: Canada and the EU are reportedly planning an economic and defense partnership.

Meta's Muse Is a Bet That Households Will Finally Pay for an Agent

Meta's new personal AI agent can fill out forms, book tables, and shop — but with a $130–145B 2026 capital budget behind it and its ad systems deliberately walled off, the real question is whether consumers will pay for help that Amazon spent $25 billion learning they wouldn't. 1% Better

Muse runs on a dedicated cloud virtual machine the user can watch, arrives via app, web, WhatsApp, and eventually Meta's glasses, and asks for a payment card before doing anything. It ties into Gmail, Spotify, Ticketmaster, and OpenTable, and can code its own integrations for apps that lack them. Free usage is limited; paid tiers run $20 and $100 a month, and Meta says Muse's conversations will never touch its ad systems.

Every agent that's actually taken hold so far has landed on payroll: OpenAI found 98% of its own employees use its Codex coding agent, versus 17% of organizational users and under 1% of individuals, because a $100 seat pays for itself the first time it saves a billable hour. No family calendar has a wage line.

Amazon already ran a version of this experiment with Alexa — a decade and 100 million homes later, its devices unit lost more than $25 billion chasing the idea that convenience alone would convert into commerce. Households took the free stuff (timers, weather) and kept the checkout for themselves.

Why it matters: Muse is the first personal agent built by a company with both the reach (roughly 3 billion WhatsApp users) and the consumer-trust architecture — isolated VM, approval gates, a separate safety agent — to find out cheaply whether a household will finally pay for an hour it's never priced.

AI & Education

Report: Students Who Use AI Frequently Do Worse on Exams

According to OECD's flagship education report, students who lean on AI for schoolwork underperform those who don't by a significant margin. Morning Brew

Students who never use AI scored 509 on a science test, versus 481 for students who use it very frequently — equivalent to about a year and a half of education once adjusted for socioeconomic status.

Still, the report notes AI can be useful if used responsibly: students who used it once or twice a week often outperformed those who used it less often, suggesting frequency and dependency matter more than access itself.

Hollywood Just Had Its Most Lucrative Summer Ever

The US domestic box office notched a record $4.76 billion haul from May through Labor Day, up almost 30% from last year and beating the prior record of $4.755 billion set in 2013. Morning Brew

Sequels ran the show: Spider-Man: Brand New Day topped the summer with $923 million domestically, while Toy Story 5 and The Devil Wears Prada 2 rode nostalgia to $480 million and $221 million. Christopher Nolan's The Odyssey, shot largely in IMAX 70mm, pulled in $589 million.

Indie horror also broke out with Gen Z: Obsession and Backrooms grossed $263 million and $197 million on budgets of $750K and $10 million, respectively. Not everything worked — Disney's $250 million live-action Moana, DC's Supergirl, and Amazon MGM's Masters of the Universe all stumbled. Adjusted for inflation, the summer box office is still down 17% from pre-Covid 2019.
