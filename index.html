The Future of Scary Movies Looks Ominously Bright

A flood of shoestring-budget hits like Barbarian and Obsession has made horror one of Hollywood’s most reliable moneymakers — and the genre is shedding its low-brow reputation right along with it. Morning Brew

Horror has historically been one of the most profitable genres in film, in part because it’s relatively cheap to churn out a decent scary movie. Based on data from 1996 to 2016, horror had the highest likelihood of profit of any genre, according to the American Film Market, with over half of all horror movies making at least $1. Morning Brew

2007’s Paranormal Activity made $193 million on a $15,000 budget. More recently, 2022’s Barbarian grossed ten times its $4.5 million budget, Smile snagged $217 million on a $17 million budget, and this year’s indie favorite Obsession — made for just $750,000 — became the first wide-release horror film to make more money in its second weekend than its first. Morning Brew

Horror fans show up no matter the broader film environment: the genre was the most consistent driver of box-office sales during the pandemic, and its success is sometimes considered a recession indicator. Morning Brew

Despite horror films starting to dip a toe into AI (and getting immediate pushback), recent hits like The Substance, Evil Dead: Burn, and Teenage Sex and Death at Camp Miasma have leaned back into good old-fashioned practical effects. Morning Brew

Hollywood’s New Scare Factory Is the Internet

Kane Parsons’s Backrooms grossed $400 million on a ~$10 million budget after starting as a short film inspired by a creepypasta image; A24 then offered him a three-year, $65 million deal — its largest ever. Fellow YouTuber Curry Barker’s sub-$1 million Obsession topped $500 million and landed him an eight-figure deal with Universal and Blumhouse. At least five more horror films based on YouTube shorts and creepypasta are now in development, with Spielberg, Jordan Peele, Warner Bros., and Lionsgate attached. Morning Brew

What Microsoft Had to Relearn With Copilot

Microsoft pulled Copilot from several Windows apps after finding high-traffic entry points weren’t delivering real value. EVP Jacob Andreou: “Usage is fleeting unless you’re delivering real value” — fewer entry points led to more usage per user. Rundown AI

Does the Model Still Matter?

Microsoft’s Jacob Andreou says no single lab wins at everything anymore — so Copilot’s new Auto mode picks the right model for the job instead of asking users to track it themselves. Rundown AI

“The model definitely matters,” Andreou said, “but having to behave as if the model really matters is a large tax to put on professionals and companies.” Earlier this year, a single provider’s full lineup could win across the board — that’s changed.

OpenAI’s 5.6-class models excel in spreadsheet work like Excel, he said, while Anthropic’s models retain a more native understanding of the visual consequences of code, making them better suited for drafting a PowerPoint deck. Copilot still lets users pick a specific model, but Auto is designed to route each task to whichever model performs best.

How Much Freedom Should an Agent Have?

Andreou’s rule of thumb for enterprise agent autonomy: sort every action into one-way doors (irreversible) and two-way doors (reversible). Rundown AI

“If an agent is contacting another person, deleting a file, or making an irreversible decision, a human needs to be in the loop every time,” he said. “For reversible work, it can operate independently inside clear boundaries and ask again if the task or risk changes.”

He described setting up the agent OpenClaw on his home network to fix things — it “found a way in” to systems it wasn’t given access to, which he called “a bit terrifying.” With Autopilot, Microsoft pre-computes task boundaries up front; if the agent’s plan changes, it has to ask for permission again.
