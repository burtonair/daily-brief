Your Job Is Not to Rush Them

It almost doesn't feel possible to take this long for a kid to get out of a car. Or for a teenager to get ready. Or to choose what they want from the store.

So what do you do? You find yourself rushing. That seems to be your role in the family — rushing everyone constantly, getting them where they're supposed to be. Guys, c'mon. Let's go. Get your stuff. We're going to be late. Hurry up.

It keeps everyone on schedule, sure, but it comes at such a high cost. They resent you. You resent them. You neglect your actual job.

Your job is not to be an efficient manager of time. Your job is not to get in and out of the toy store quickly, or to set land-speed records. Your job is to be their parent. Your job is to be present. Your job is to build trust and connection and to have fun together. Dawdle. Linger. Slow down. Let it take as long as it takes.

Try ChatGPT's New Voices with GPT-Live-1.1

Try ChatGPT's new voices, compare them against the same short passage, and check whether a spoken correction actually reaches the final answer.

  1. Open ChatGPT's Voice settings and choose Live where available. Pick a voice and start a new chat with a fictional workshop notice.
  2. Ask it to read the notice, then try the same words with another voice. Keep the language and instructions fixed so the comparison stays useful.
  3. Try a regional voice in a language you know well. Listen for clear names, dates, and numbers, and ask for a slower repeat when needed.
  4. Interrupt the notice with a new date and headcount. Check that those details change while the time and budget stay fixed.
  5. Ask Voice to search OpenAI's release notes. Open its source link and check the answer rather than trusting the spoken search cue alone.

Pro tip: A new voice and a new model are different changes — comparing one voice against your usual one doesn't prove a gain over the underlying model. Full guide.

Supreme Court Agrees to Hear Home-Distilling Case

The justices will reconsider a 160-year-old federal ban on home liquor distillation, agreeing to hear a challenge from a group of Texas home distillers. Morning Brew

The federal government began taxing distilled spirits in the late 1700s to help pay for the Revolutionary War, then banned home distillation outright in 1868, arguing most home spirit-makers were evading taxes. The ban carried penalties of up to $10,000 in fines and five years in prison — and even if it's struck down, distillers would still owe tax on what they make.

One federal appeals court has already deemed the ban unconstitutional; another upheld it after a similar suit. The Supreme Court is expected to rule by next June.
