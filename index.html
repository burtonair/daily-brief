Claude Helps Solve Math's Biggest Open Problem

Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge says he and Claude just proved the Riemann hypothesis — a 167-year-old prime-number puzzle carrying a $1M Millennium Prize — though critics say no human fully understands the proof. The Rundown AI

First posed in 1859, the Riemann hypothesis asks whether prime numbers follow a predictable pattern, and the Clay Institute attached a $1M "Millennium" prize to solving it in 2000. Alpöge spent roughly six weeks working with Claude before sharing the proof — all 17 million lines of machine-checkable math — writing that Claude "flew, and danced, and sparkled near that finish line."

The group behind Lean, the verification language used to check the proof, certified it over the weekend, saying they have "full confidence" it's correct. Not everyone's convinced it should count: the Association for Human Mathematics called it "little more than publicity" for AI labs, since no human can fully follow it. Hours later, a rival, human-only proof from Alessandro Masullo surfaced — though others quickly flagged problems with it.

Top mathematicians had expected the problem to take decades of new theory to crack. The practical payoff may be limited for now, but once this speed of progress spreads to fields like biology and drug design, it won't stay academic for long.

Seize This Moment With Them

Every parent's deepest fear is losing their child. And the terrible, beautiful tragedy of parenthood is that, indeed, we are constantly losing our children. Day by day by day.

Not literally, of course, but in the sense that they are growing, changing, becoming something new, something more independent — on a daily, if not an hourly, basis. On the Daily Stoic podcast, Professor Scott Galloway talked about the profound grief he felt looking at a picture of his 11-year-old. Yes, it was true that that 11-year-old was now a great 14-year-old — but the 11-year-old was no more.

Such is our fate. Such is the life we signed up for. We want them to grow. We can't wait for them to start walking, to start school, to experience all the wonderful things life has in store for them. Yet this also means they'll never again be what they are right now — that what they are right now is ephemeral and fleeting for us at best. Blink, get distracted, take it for granted? It's gone. You've missed it.

Remember: tempus fugit. Time flies. Seize it, and hold onto them while you can. Seize it before you lose it — and them — forever.

How To | The Rundown AI

Test Gemini 4 Neon on Finding Billing Errors

Today's AI Training guide puts Google's new Gemini 4 Neon to work on an everyday job: reading a year of electricity bills against your bank's payment history to find why a bill jumped, then drafting the dispute email.

  1. Download a year of electricity bills as PDFs and your bank's payment history as a CSV; delete any bank row that isn't a payment to the utility.
  2. Start a temporary chat in the Gemini app, choose Deeper analysis (4 Neon) from the model menu, and set Thinking level to High.
  3. Attach both files and ask for a month-by-month table of usage, rate, and total — plus which month jumped and why.
  4. Ask where each problem appears in the source files (the PDF page/line, or the bank row), and check two or three of those spots yourself.
  5. Ask for a short dispute email citing every date and amount, check it against the files, then send it to the utility.

Pro tip: ask for the evidence before you act on an answer — a model that names the page, line, or row is easy to check, and only takes a minute.

OpenAI Opens Its Private AI Scorecard to Rivals

A rare look inside OpenAI's own intelligence benchmark — and a public call for industry-wide limits. The Rundown AI

OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki published the internal scores the company uses to track its models' raw intelligence since GPT-3.5, and asked rival labs to agree on shared limits to hold until better AI-monitoring tools exist.

The metric, log-perplexity, measures how well a model predicts the next token — lower is better. Pachocki tied OpenAI's biggest recent gains in cybersecurity and math to scores below 0.8, down from about 0.99 for the original ChatGPT model, and said an unreleased internal model is already at roughly 0.73. He expects "further transformative capabilities" in the 0.68–0.7 range.

Sam Altman called the post "the most important thing OpenAI has published in a while," saying agreeing on common limits "would be very wise." It echoes a similar METR report on Anthropic — the data increasingly suggests capability acceleration isn't slowing down.
