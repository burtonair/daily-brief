Global Bond Rout Deepens as Iran War Drives Oil Toward $100

Government debt is getting dumped worldwide as investors, spooked by inflation and rising fiscal deficits, push yields to multidecade highs — with 10-year Treasurys hitting levels last seen in 2002. Morning Brew

Some of yesterday's spikes reversed by the close, but at their peaks: the 10-year US Treasury yield hit 5.34% (highest since 2002), France's 10-year matched its own 2002 high, Britain's 30-year topped 6% for the first time since 1998, and Japan's 10-year held at 30-year record levels. Investors are "picking between winners and losers," per the Wall Street Journal — US and German yields fell while French, Italian, and Greek yields stayed elevated. Read more Morning Brew

The trigger: the Iran war has disrupted global energy flows, pushing the international oil benchmark from roughly $70 a barrel before the war to around $100 now, exacerbating inflation worldwide. Rockefeller Capital Management's chief investment officer told the Journal to expect volatility to persist, and countries with ballooning deficits — France chief among them — now look "borderline radioactive" to bond investors as fragmented political leaders scramble for spending cuts. Why it matters: US debt just topped $40 trillion, but investors have stayed comparatively calm on short-term Treasurys after softer-than-expected inflation data made a second rate hike this year look less likely. This morning's jobs report could sharpen or ease that picture. Morning Brew

Can You Say "No" for Your Kids?

At the beginning of our careers, we have to say "yes." We have to say "yes" a lot — to the late nights, the unpleasant assignments, the trips, the work other people don't want to do. We have to jump on every opportunity. We can't turn down business.

We do this until we make it, until we get to a certain position of success or power or leverage. We do this until eventually we have the ability, even the necessity, to say "no."

Then we have kids, and that's one of the things that requires us to say "no." The question is, after years of building that other habit, can you start doing it? Can you start saying "no"? Can you say "no" for them?

Because at some point, success stops being about how much you can take on. It becomes about what you're willing to turn down — the meeting, the trip, the extra money. Your kids need you to say "yes" to them, and sometimes the only way to do that is to say "no" to everything else. Daily Dad
