Anthropic's Threat Report Catches Chinese Labs Serving Claude as Their Own

A 150+ page report details eight months of Claude misuse Anthropic shut down globally — seven Chinese labs running distillation schemes, with Moonshot and DeepSeek caught passing off Claude's own answers as their models' work. The Rundown AI Morning Brew

The report. Anthropic published its latest Threat Report, detailing the most notable cases of Claude misuse it disrupted over the past eight months — from bioweapon flags and espionage to Chinese AI labs quietly running Claude in place of their own models.

Named and shamed. Anthropic identified seven Chinese labs behind distillation efforts, including Alibaba, DeepSeek, Moonshot, and Xiaomi, leaning on thousands of fraudulent accounts. Moonshot and DeepSeek went further, Anthropic said, serving Claude directly to their own customers and passing its answers off as their own model's output, then using the responses for training.

Beyond distillation. Five biology cases from scientists using Claude raised flags for possible weapons applications (Anthropic said it "does not assert that they intended harm"), one Yemen-based operation used Claude Code to build rocket guidance software, and a consultant built Mali's spy agency a system aiming to watch 25 million phone lines. Anthropic also said it had blocked efforts to use Claude for bioweapons research.

Why it matters. Every case in the report was pulled off with Opus-level models and below — a reminder that the scale of misuse only gets harder to police as more capable models ship, and fresh scrutiny on how easily rivals can ride on the back of frontier labs' own research.

You Can Always Give Them Five More Minutes

They were traveling with their three-year-old son. They boarded their flight in Boston, heading back to Los Angeles after visiting family in Cape Cod. They could not have anticipated that just 49 minutes into the trip, their plane would be hijacked and driven into the south tower of the World Trade Center.

They could not have known this, just as they could not have known that not long before that flight, two fathers were taking their son, little David Reed Gamboa Brandhorst, to the playground in West Hollywood Park for the last time. After a wonderful afternoon, they told David it was time to go, and he shouted back, "Just five more minutes, Daddy." And they relented, as they always did. They just didn't know it was the last time.

None of us do. There's a last time you change a diaper, a last time you push them in a stroller or walk them to school. There's a last meal, a last hug. Inevitably, it will catch us by surprise — even though we know our kids are growing up, even though we know everyone and everything that's ever been born will die. It surprises us because we don't know when, and because we love them, and love life, so much that it always comes too soon.

Which is why, when our kids ask for a few more minutes, for one more hug, for a ride, for anything, we should find a way to give it to them. Because we're giving it to ourselves, too. On this 25th anniversary, the inscription on the monument to David at the park where he used to play reads:

"May David's playground always be a place of joy, laughter and safe haven for every little boy and girl who asks for just one more trip down the big slide — or as David would say, 'Just five more minutes, Daddy.'"

How Realistic Is Trump's Plan to Send Everyone $5K?

Trump promised every American adult $5,000 if the GOP holds Congress in November — but lawmakers and economists from both parties are questioning the cost, the legality, and the inflation risk. Morning Brew

Critics flagged the plan's achievability, legality, and potential to push up inflation the way Covid stimulus did — especially with the US already carrying $40 trillion in debt.

The plan would cost roughly $1.2 trillion. Vance said tariff revenue would cover it, but import duties brought in only $300 billion from January 2025 through July 2026, and $166 billion of that is earmarked to refund companies after SCOTUS nixed most of Trump's tariffs.

Legality is contested — critics say it violates the federal ban on payments to induce voting, others call it a permissible campaign promise — and Congress would have to approve it, with bipartisan backlash making enough support unclear.
