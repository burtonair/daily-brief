Meta Agrees to $18 Billion Settlement Over Child Safety

Meta agreed to pay as much as $18 billion to settle a federal lawsuit brought by California and other states accusing the company of designing its platforms to keep children addicted and collecting data from kids under 13 without parental consent. Morning Brew

Meta will pay the 47 states involved $12.7 billion over the next 10 years, plus another $5.3 billion only if TikTok and YouTube also adopt similar safeguards and kick in matching sums of their own. Meta did not admit wrongdoing. As part of the deal, Instagram and Facebook will cap teens aged 13–17 at two hours a day, block access from midnight to 6am, mute push notifications during school hours, and hide likes and reactions from minors by default.

The company said it expects to book a roughly $10 billion legal expense in Q3 tied to the agreement, which could pressure its ad business as fewer teens spend late-night hours scrolling. Still, the deal removes an existential threat: states had been seeking up to $1.4 trillion — close to Meta's entire market cap. Meta shares finished slightly up on the news.

Florida is not part of the deal; its attorney general has sued separately, and Meta still faces other suits from individuals and school districts it plans to fight.

Columnist | Daily Dad

You Are Already Rich

Of course you want to make more. Of course you want to be secure. Of course you want to be paid well, be able to afford nice things, be able to take care of your family, even spoil them a little. You want to be rich—who doesn't? That's why we work hard, why we fight hard, why we've been and want to continue to be successful, professionally and financially. But as we chase wealth, we often forget that we are rich.

Mark Twain, who chased money his whole career, was devastated when his daughter tragically died while he was on an international lecture tour—he never forgave himself for not being there. He compared her to a bank treasure whose value he hadn't recognized until it vanished in the night. "The bank is broken, my fortune is gone," he told a friend, "I am a pauper."

Twain didn't understand, as he moved from one scheme to the next, one big project and luxury item to the next, that he already had everything he needed. He had something priceless—which he ignored in favor of something much less important and meaningful.

He's not the only one to do that—we all do. We say we're "doing this for our family," and yet what does our work take us away from, require us to be apart from? Our family. In chasing riches, we ignore the riches right here, right now, in front of us. What an impoverishing thing. Daily Dad

A Beginner's Guide to ChatGPT Work

Set up a ChatGPT Work project for a recurring workflow: process the work once, then automate it.

  1. Create a local folder with a two-digit prefix. In ChatGPT desktop, open New chat > Work > Choose project > New project, name it, and pick that folder as the source.
  2. Tell ChatGPT about your project and ask it to propose a structure.
  3. Add in some input documents and tell ChatGPT to process them once.
  4. Once it's done, tell it to create three useful automations that run on a schedule to automate the processing going forward.

Pro tip: Tell ChatGPT to turn those automated routines into skills you can call any time manually with a slash command. Full guide.

Bill Gates Warns "There Is No Plan" for AI Transition

One of tech's greatest innovators rang one of the loudest AI alarms yet: Bill Gates wrote in a nearly 6,000-word essay that he doesn't "see evidence that leaders, experts, and communities are confronting the challenges adequately." Morning Brew

Gates argues the threat AI and robotics pose to blue- and white-collar jobs isn't comparable to past technological transitions, which unfolded over generations. He urged governments to tax AI tokens and bots, set aside certain jobs for humans, and cooperate internationally on regulation—especially between the US and China. "I don't really know anybody who's not concerned," he told Axios, saying many of the largest tech companies are privately worried about what they might create.
