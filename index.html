Has Youth Sports Lost the Plot? Private Equity Cashes In on the Boom

Over the past two decades, private equity has transformed youth sports into a cutthroat, $40 billion-a-year industrial complex — expensive enough to break family budgets and controversial enough to become a possible 2028 campaign issue. Morning Brew

The backdrop. When news broke that Kim Kardashian would produce a TV show following the parents of elite teen baseball players, the internet's reaction was blunt: this is the last thing kids need. Private equity has transformed youth sports into an industry cutthroat enough to cause a shortage of willing umpires and referees, and expensive enough to break the bank.

Case study. One Florida parent who recalls playing rec sports for $80 per season as a child now spends $8,000 per year on her 12-year-old son's club baseball practices, tournaments, and equipment, she told the Washington Post. Family spending on youth sports leaped 46% from 2019 to 2024, making it a $40 billion-a-year industry — more than the combined annual revenue of the NFL and NBA, per the Aspen Institute. Financial strain has caused 1 in 5 parents to limit or end their kids' participation, per a New York Life survey.

The players. After budget cuts left gaps for investors to fill, private equity went on a spree buying up teams, facilities, software, and media rights: Black Bear Sports Group, the nation's largest hockey rink owner-operator; Varsity Brands, the KKR-owned cheerleading giant that ballooned from $1.5B to $4.5B in valuation; and Unrivaled Sports, a new venture chasing dominance in youth flag football.

Why it matters. There's little evidence pay-to-play leagues actually produce elite athletes, and some research suggests early specialization raises injury risk. Connecticut Sen. Chris Murphy is now pushing a bill to limit private equity's place in kids' sports — a sign the issue could reach the 2028 campaign trail.

Girls Flag Football Is on a Remarkable Run

High school girls' flag football participation jumped from under 16,000 to roughly 102,000 in four years, and girls ages 6–12 playing the sport rose 283% since 2015. The sport's growth is being fueled in part by the Atlanta Falcons' ownership, and it makes its Olympic debut in LA in 2028. Morning Brew

Yes, You Can Bet on Little League Baseball Games

US sportsbooks and Kalshi/Polymarket won't touch it, but offshore site BetOnline says demand for Little League World Series markets outstrips the WNBA and golf. Little League International's response: "No one should be exploiting the success and failures of children." Morning Brew

The US Hobby Horse Championships

400+ stick horse competitors and 800+ spectators gathered in Michigan last month for a sport dominated by girls and young women. Morning Brew
