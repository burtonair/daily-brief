Anthropic Researcher's Resignation Reignites the AI 'Extinction' Debate

Jacob Coxon quit, warning that both OpenAI and Anthropic are "gambling with our lives." Then Anthropic's own Alignment Science lead said he believes there's a greater-than-10% chance AI kills everyone within a decade — and the debate went from fringe to front page. The Rundown AI Morning Brew

The resignation. Jacob Coxon, who worked at both OpenAI and Anthropic, resigned this week and wrote in a post with 110+ million views that "the people building AI earnestly believe that it could kill us all by the end of the decade. This is not a marketing stunt." He said many at OpenAI "have not deeply internalized the civilizational stakes," but the fears are "well-understood" at Anthropic — which, he argued, is still "locked in a race" it believes it must win because "no one else will act responsibly."

The reply that lit the fuse. Anthropic's Alignment Science lead Evan Hubinger responded on X: "We really do earnestly believe AI could kill all humans! I personally think it is >10% within the next decade." Hubinger later clarified that today's models are low-risk and the real danger is self-improving AI, for which there's no control plan yet.

Not the first. Senior safety researchers at Anthropic, OpenAI, and xAI resigned for similar reasons earlier this year, Bill Gates issued a stark warning last month, and OpenAI's chief scientist has separately called for a development slowdown — one some members of Congress are also weighing how to enforce.

Why it matters. Anthropic markets itself as the safety-conscious lab. Having its own alignment lead publicly hand a number to the odds of human extinction doesn't inspire confidence that anyone — including the safety team — has a plan for what comes next.

To Protect and Benefit Them

In a famous exchange, the King of Syria asked the Stoic philosopher Musonius Rufus whether it was appropriate for a leader to study philosophy. Not only is it appropriate, Musonius answered, but for no one is it more appropriate. A leader's duty, he said, is "to be able to protect and benefit his people" — through situations they've never faced before.

This is also exactly what it means to be a parent. A parent makes a commitment to protecting and benefiting their children. "The person who intends to protect and help people," Musonius says, "must know what is good for a human being and what is bad, what is helpful and what is harmful, what is useful and what is useless."

Where does one go to arm themselves with that knowledge? Books. Harry Truman said not all readers are leaders, but all leaders are readers — and it's equally true that not all readers are great parents, but all great parents are readers. We have to read to have something to offer our children: aid in learning good from bad, helpful from harmful, useful from useless. We have to read to lead.

Why Is Everyone Mad at Stella Lefty?

The 24-year-old country-pop singer (real name Lefkofsky) keeps getting accused of copying hits from Noah Kahan to Miley Cyrus — and her billionaire Groupon-founder dad isn't helping the "nepo baby" narrative. Morning Brew

Sports & Business

The NFL Brought a Spreadsheet's Worth of Logistics to Australia

The Rams and 49ers played the NFL's first regular-season game in Australia, hauling 90 trunks of equipment and navigating a 17-hour time difference — all in service of growing the league's international revenue. Morning Brew

The two teams took opposite approaches to jet lag: the 49ers left San Francisco over a week early to acclimate, while the Rams flew in about 24 hours before kickoff, opting to sleep on the 16-hour flight instead.

Australia's strict biodiversity rules meant every piece of gear had to be scrubbed of grass and dirt before customs — no small task for 90 trunks weighing roughly 25,000 pounds, on top of a shipping container of food and supplies the Rams sent over months ago.

The 10:35am local kickoff was a business decision as much as a football one: the NFL is chasing international revenue and fanbase growth, and Melbourne was this year's proving ground.
