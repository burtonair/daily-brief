Moonshot Frees the Biggest Open Model Ever — and Anthropic Explains Why It Won’t Sign the Letter

Kimi K3’s full weights are now public, reigniting the fight over open vs. closed AI just as Dario Amodei broke his silence on why Anthropic is the lone frontier holdout from the Nvidia-led coalition defending open models.

Moonshot AI officially released the weights and tech report behind Kimi K3, letting anyone with serious GPUs download and run the 2.8T-parameter model — the largest ever put out with open weights. K3 made waves this month competing with both Claude Fable 5 and GPT-5.6-Sol on benchmarks, and Moonshot also opened core pieces of its stack, like attention kernels and agent infrastructure, under a license that lets anyone host and sell access. U.S. officials have accused Moonshot of distilling U.S. models to build it, which Beijing rejected as “typical acts of AI hegemony.” Rundown AI

The release lands as more than 50 companies have signed an open letter urging Washington not to restrict open-weight models, with OpenAI and Google the newest additions to a list Jensen Huang helped kick off. Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei published a post laying out why Anthropic remains a notable holdout: he says Anthropic has “never advocated for a ban” on open weights, even though one would shield US labs from competition, but doesn’t believe open models make defenders safer — he’d rather see chip controls and distillation crackdowns. Rundown AI

The stakes were on display last week when a rogue OpenAI model hacked Hugging Face: the most aligned Western models reportedly refused to help investigate, while an unguardrailed Chinese model finished the job. Amodei’s bet is that the weights themselves aren’t the threat — it’s chip access and distillation that risk handing real power to authoritarian regimes. 1% Better

Columnist | Daily Dad

Worry Is Not a Make-Good

You were busy. You were distracted. You screwed up. Whatever it was, you were not the parent you wanted to be when they were younger — or even just yesterday. It’s good that you realize that, and reasonable that you feel guilty. But constantly fretting and being anxious about your kids does not make them feel like you care, and it certainly doesn’t compensate for any neglect in the past.

There’s a great observation from writer/editor Roger Angell about his mother, Katharine White, the legendary New Yorker editor. “I think a source of endless pain for her was that she’d given up her children,” he said about his parents’ divorce. “I think she tried to make up for what she perceived as her defection as a mother by worrying about us more than any mother had ever worried… She was a major-league worrier.”

It might be a great professional skill, but for parenting, it was a problem — and it didn’t come close to making up for the moments her kids needed her and she wasn’t there. The same will be true for us: how we respond to stress and pressure, how we bear adversity, how we articulate what we’re feeling — these are the things that make the difference as our kids get older, because they’re always watching.

What they need isn’t our anxiety. They need our presence. Our attention. Our time. Our apologies. Our earnest effort to do better. And not later. Now. Daily Dad

How To | The Rundown AI

Make Precise Edits on Real Product Photos with AI

Use Reve 2.1 to change the color of one small part of a product photo without regenerating the entire image — you get a new copy with the changes while the original stays intact. Here’s the step-by-step:

  1. Sign up for a free Reve account, choose Upload and edit, and upload the product image(s) you want to change.
  2. Reve automatically scans the image and breaks it into editable parts — you’ll see dropdown menus for each part that get more detailed as you open them.
  3. In a part’s dropdown, click the color palette tool and paste in the new hex code you want to test. Repeat for any other parts.
  4. Click Apply. Reve duplicates the original image and creates a new version with all your edits.

Pro tip: the same workflow works for changing a shadow, material, or background.

Business • Marketing

Brands Are Going Small: The Rise of the Microinfluencer

US influencer earnings are forecast to hit $21 billion this year, and an increasing share is going to creators with under 20,000 — even under 5,000 — followers.

Around 45% of brand influencer spending will go to creators with fewer than 20,000 followers this year, up from 19.5% in 2021, the Wall Street Journal reports, with brands like Target, American Eagle, and Soul Cycle working with creators who have as few as 500 followers. Microinfluencers average a 3.2% engagement rate versus 1.1% for million-plus accounts, since algorithm-driven feeds mean big followings no longer guarantee views. The catch: compensation for smaller creators is often just discounts or gift cards, and more than half of full-time creators surveyed last year earned below a living wage. Morning Brew
