Frontier AI Staff Fear Their Own Internal Models

Insiders at Anthropic and OpenAI no longer believe their unreleased models can be shipped safely, with staff debating whether future frontier AI should stay in-house — or be built at all. The Rundown AI

Axios reported that many staffers at Anthropic and OpenAI no longer think their unreleased models can be shipped safely, citing over two dozen sources. Internal models have reportedly gotten so strong across domains that staff testing them find them hard — or even impossible — to control, and some have shown signs of hiding their reasoning from safety monitors.

Anthropic pushed back through a spokesperson, saying "Keeping a model internal doesn't make it safe," while OpenAI said new safety rules could slow its releases. OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki proposed on Sunday that labs agree on shared limits they won't cross until tools for watching AI behavior catch up.

Between Anthropic's mystery "Model 4" in METR's recent report and the breakthroughs coming from internal systems, it's hard to know where the capability ceiling sits inside the top labs. Safe public releases are one worry, but the other is a few companies keeping far more powerful AI to themselves behind closed doors.

We Have To Take Care of This

Our parents took care of us. They made sure we were clothed and fed. They sacrificed. They fought. They gave us a better life. That's what families do, right? That's the biological imperative.

But it can be so easy to forget how many other people helped us when we were small — people who had no genetic imperative to do so. Think of the teachers who taught you, the coaches who trained you, the people who encouraged you. Think of the people who fought to establish those public schools in the first place, and to integrate them. Think of the people who invented the treatments that allowed you to survive infancy. Think of the strangers who stepped in and stopped you from running into the street — or did so for an ancestor without whom you would not exist.

We belong to a thing called society. We are in this together. We have to fight and take care of our kids, but we cannot forget our obligations to all kids. We cannot neglect the future. We cannot excuse ourselves from our duty to the stranger, to the unfortunate, to the orphan or the abused.

Just as we may never know the people who paved the way for us, the people we help may never know our names. That's not the point. We benefited from the sacrifices of others. Now it's our turn to make those sacrifices — not just for our kids, but for all kids.

Go Beyond the Browser With the Claude Desktop App

Today's AI Training guide walks through the three places Microsoft rebuilt Copilot around: Home for quick questions, Code for building small apps, and Autopilot as an agent that works toward a goal for weeks in Teams and Outlook.

  1. Open Copilot, sign in with your work account, choose Chat in the message box on Home, and ask what's on your calendar tomorrow
  2. Select Code at the top of the left pane, switch the Local menu to cloud, and describe a tracker for your team's open requests
  3. Type "Publish it," and ask Code to share the tracker with your team and confirm whether they can use it or edit it
  4. Select Autopilot to create your agent. Name it, give it a goal with a deadline, check its summary of your work, and approve its plan
  5. In a Teams thread, type @ and your Autopilot's name, then give it a job, like drafting a decision doc from the thread

Pro tip: Pick the place by how long the job lasts — a question takes seconds in Chat, a tool you'll reuse belongs in Code, and work that runs for weeks goes to Autopilot.

xAI Founding Trio Builds a "Time Machine" for AI Forecasting

Three xAI founding members launched Epochal, a startup giving AI agents a time machine to relive 30+ years of the web, aiming to unlock superhuman forecasters.

Guodong Zhang, Zihang Dai and Manuel Kroiss launched Epochal and debuted Time Portal, the first piece of what it calls a "time machine for the internet," built in just 90 days. The 1T+ page archive saves busy pages as often as once a minute, so agents can drop into a specific hour, not just a day, back in time — even the agent's own computer gets set back, clock and software downloads matched to the chosen date so it can't spot the trick.

Agents forecast 347 Kalshi and Polymarket questions live in testing, and Time Portal replays weeks later made nearly the same calls. With enough data on the past, the thinking goes, the future starts to look predictable.

AI & Music

Tidal Draws a Hard Line on AI-Made Music

Block-owned Tidal will delete every track it detects as fully AI-generated starting Dec. 9 and refuse new submissions — the first subscription streaming service to move from labels to an outright ban.

Tidal announced it expects to reject 80,000–85,000 new uploads daily once the ban starts, and said AI tracks made up 43% of all submissions to the platform since late August. Tracks made with AI assistance are still allowed, using detection tech from partner Ircam Amplify plus metadata to catch songs made exclusively by AI.

Tidal lead Robert Andersen called the company the first streaming service to take this step: "We encourage our peers to follow suit." Spotify tags AI acts as "AI Personas" and has deleted 75M spam tracks, while Apple Music labels AI-generated songs — but neither bans them completely.
