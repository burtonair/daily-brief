It Only Takes a Moment

In 2006, Benjamin Mee bought a zoo. Literally a zoo. It was broken down and in desperate need of a caring owner. Mee and his family were struggling too.

In one scene — immortalized by Matt Damon in the movie version of Mee's book, We Bought a Zoo — Mee explains to his son that our lives are defined by the moments when we put ourselves out there. When we take a risk that, if we had thought about too much or been too deliberate about, we'd never have been capable of taking. "You know," he said, "sometimes all you need is twenty seconds of insane courage. Just literally twenty seconds of just embarrassing bravery. And I promise you, something great will come of it."

This idea of breaking courage down into little pieces is a very good one for us parents to pass on to our own kids. A person isn't brave, generally. We can only be brave, specifically, in the moment. This is as true for us and our kids or Benjamin Mee's son as it is for the most decorated soldiers who have ever served in the military.

If you read the citations for many Medal of Honor recipients, the action that rises to the level of heroism is almost always just a moment. It's usually not the fighting off of 12 insurgents for 5 hours — it's the sprinting across an open plain for 20 seconds, exposed to enemy gunfire on three sides, to come to the aid of a fallen comrade. So teach them to give themselves a few seconds of courage. Tell them something great will come of it. Promise them.

Make Claude Code Check Its Work After Each Edit

Build a hook that formats a settings file and runs tests automatically after Claude makes selected edits — so a failed check catches a bug before it ships, without you having to remember to look.

  1. Open a new practice folder and ask Claude to build the guide's small shipping calculator. Run the five tests to confirm it works before adding a hook.
  2. Add a hook that formats the settings file after Claude writes it, then confirm it makes the settings easier to read without changing the values.
  3. Add the shipping tests to the same hook, so formatting finishes before the checks run, then confirm all five pass.
  4. Ask Claude to introduce the guide's deliberate error so an order worth exactly $50 fails the free-shipping test. Read the failure before fixing anything.
  5. Restore the original rule, rerun the same tests, confirm all five pass, then follow the guide's steps to turn the hook off.

Pro tip: Keep the tests unchanged while fixing the bug. The hook checks an edit after it saves, so a failed check leaves the change in place. Full guide.

Anthropic's Claude Takes on Scorpion Antivenom

An internal Anthropic AI designed proteins to lock onto and disarm scorpion venom toxins, in a project run with investment firm Citrini Research — 17 of 211 lab-tested designs actually worked. The Rundown AI

  • Citrini's five staffers worked out of Anthropic's offices on an estimated $200,000 budget, after they couldn't get a safety block lifted on their own machines.
  • Claude ran for 64 hours off five human messages, drawing up 318,484 candidate designs and sending 211 out for lab work at Twist Bioscience and Adaptyv Bio.
  • 17 designs passed binding tests, but each grabs only one toxin, and none have been tested to see if they actually stop the venom.

Citrini called its moonshot of a universal antivenom "failed" — but its takeaway was that the frontier for AI-driven drug discovery has moved, especially given who was holding the keyboard: investment-newsletter staffers with no deep drug-development experience. Full story.
