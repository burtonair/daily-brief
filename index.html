Lawmakers Take Aim at the Ghost Jobs Haunting Job Seekers

Roughly one in five online job listings each quarter are "ghost jobs" that will never lead to a hire, and state lawmakers from New York to California are moving to force employers to disclose real hiring timelines. Morning Brew

The government can't do anything about your Hinge matches — unless they run a company posting about roles that may not actually be open, aka "ghost jobs." These listings, which attract applicants but lead to no hiring activity, are running so rampant that lawmakers across the country are working to get 'em banned, the Wall Street Journal reported. Your résumé isn't chopped — you may just be applying to jobs that employers have already filled or never intended to fill. Roughly one in five listings advertised on online job boards per quarter are ghost jobs, according to the hiring platform Greenhouse, and about 81% of recruiters said in a 2024 MyPerfectResume survey that their employers posted ghost job ads.

New York state legislators recently passed a bill (now awaiting the governor's signature) that would fine employers who don't mention expected hiring timelines in job postings; New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Kentucky, and California are considering similar reforms. Texas is going after the middleman instead — Attorney General Ken Paxton opened an investigation into LinkedIn last month, alleging the platform promotes and profits from ghost jobs.

Why do employers post them? Best case, an approved opening got nixed and nobody took down the listing in time. Worse case: 38% of recruiters say they post fake jobs to stay relevant on job boards when they're not hiring, and 36% do it to test a listing's effectiveness, per the MyPerfectResume survey.

Seoul Dethrones Dubai as the World's Busiest International Airport

Turmoil in the Middle East has ended Dubai's 12-year reign atop international air travel, with Seoul's Incheon Airport serving 38.4 million international travelers in the first half of the year. Morning Brew

Since the Iran war started in February, the shift away from Dubai has been drastic: international passengers there fell from 7.4 million in February to 2.5 million in March, recovering only to 4.7 million by June. Transfer traffic through the Middle East has fallen by half since the war began, per the New York Times, with much of it rerouting through Asia — Incheon saw an 18% rise in connecting flyers and a 63% jump in passengers connecting onward to Europe.

The International Air Transport Association reported bookings down in the Middle East for June through September, but up 33% year-over-year in Asia-Pacific for the same stretch.
