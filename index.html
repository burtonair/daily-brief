FIFA Drops Plan to Sell a $4.2 Billion Stake in the World Cup

Facing a revolt from UEFA and his own COO, FIFA president Gianni Infantino scrapped a proposal to sell a 20% stake in World Cup profits to a private equity group led by Joshua Kushner.

FIFA President Gianni Infantino officially recalled his proposal to sell a 20% stake in future World Cup profits to private equity, after resounding condemnation from inside and outside the sport. The plan would have spun off FIFA’s money-making arms — tournament proceeds, broadcast deals, hospitality — into a subsidiary valued at $20 billion, with a 20% stake sold to a group led by Joshua Kushner (brother of President Trump’s son-in-law) for $4.2 billion. Morning Brew

The backlash was fierce: UEFA vowed to boycott all FIFA events unless the plan was scrapped, the Asian Football Confederation joined the North American, Central American, and Caribbean federations in denouncing it, and Infantino’s own advisor — a former Goldman Sachs banker — quit, calling it “a bad deal.” FIFA’s COO told the AP that Infantino had “deceived” staff, calling it “the project of one person.” Morning Brew

Infantino announced Friday evening that FIFA was backing away from the deal: “Our purpose has always been — and will always be — to unite and improve. As a result, this proposal will not proceed.” It remains unclear whether Infantino himself will stick around — he had seemed poised to coast into a fourth term after a successful North American World Cup. Your Roomba Is Now a National Security Threat

The FCC banned new foreign-made robovacs, humanoid robots, and delivery bots this week — and China isn’t happy about it.

The FCC issued a ban on new, foreign-made humanoid robots, quadrupeds, and other bots that can recognize their surroundings, including robovacs and sidewalk delivery robots. China, which controls about 85% of the global humanoid robot market per Barclays, threatened retaliation, saying the ban “severely damages China–US economic and trade stability.” Morning Brew

The Trump administration cited privacy concerns — foreign-made robots collecting sensitive data in homes and public spaces — plus supply-chain risk. But the Verge noted the ban doesn’t target specific companies or curb data collection directly, and US robotics firms that rely on Chinese parts worry it could obliterate their own supply chains.
