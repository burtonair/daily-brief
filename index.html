OpenAI's Math Avalanche Divides Mathematicians — and Previews AI's Next Target: Biology

OpenAI dumped solutions to 372 previously unsolved math problems this week, a release so aggressive that 25 Fields Medal winners signed a letter accusing the company of rushing to publish uncredited proofs. Meanwhile, Mark Zuckerberg's Biohub just expanded its own moonshot into a $1.8B effort to make AI simulate living cells — a sign math's "AI moment" is about to repeat itself in biology. Morning Brew Rundown AI

OpenAI's 722-page release of findings claims advancements in three of the five Millennium Prize Problems — the notoriously hard math questions that come with a $1 million bounty — across number theory, algebraic geometry, and topology. Some mathematicians are spiraling at how fast the models power through problems that have stumped humans for a century; others note the AI's proof leaned on a controversial loophole and an "unnatural" approach. Morning Brew

The field was already on edge: last month OpenAI said it solved the Navier-Stokes Equation, another Millennium Problem, and declined to claim the prize money. On Sept. 25, 25 Fields Medal winners signed a letter accusing AI companies of rushing to publish uncredited proofs, warning the pace could push mathematicians to keep research private and slow progress instead of speeding it up. Morning Brew

"Math is currently in the thick of its AI moment," Rundown AI notes — and Mark Zuckerberg wants biology to be next. His and Priscilla Chan's Biohub has grown its Virtual Biology Initiative from $500M to $1.8B, with the NIH, the Energy Department, Google DeepMind, Meta, and drug startup Isomorphic Labs all feeding in data toward a "universal virtual cell" that could predict how cells react to a drug before a single lab test. Biohub's science head says getting there will take data on trillions of cells, versus the hundreds of millions in today's biggest sets. Rundown AI

Give Claude's Routine Work to Haiku 5.5

Use Haiku 5.5 on a routine Claude task like sorting customer messages, then ask Sonnet to review the exceptions — checking sources and comparing the full workflow's cost before using it on real work. Full guide here.

  1. Open a clean practice folder in Claude Code and add the guide's sample messages. Define the categories and the requests that need a person's review.
  2. Keep Sonnet as your main model. Ask it to create a Haiku helper that can read the sample file and suggest routes, without editing files or contacting anyone.
  3. Have the helper sort one small batch. Check the task details to confirm Haiku ran, then make sure every message appears once with a quote from its source.
  4. Give Sonnet the flagged messages and their original text. Ask for review notes and missing information. Leave refunds, replies, and account changes to a person.
  5. Compare the result with a Sonnet-only run. Count errors, editing time, and usage across both stages before choosing which approach to keep.

Pro tip: Start with one helper for a small batch rather than one agent per message.

Nate's Notebook: Slop Grenades Trickle Down

Shopify CEO Tobi Lütke's team has a name for AI-generated reports that look polished and say almost nothing: slop grenades. Rundown educator Nate Grahek argues the worst ones are thrown by leaders, not employees. Rundown AI

Lütke's sharpest question — "why did we invent decompression and recompression?" — captures the problem: BetterUp and Stanford found 40% of U.S. desk workers received a slop grenade in a single month, costing them about two hours each to decode.

Nate's prescription: leaders should go first, transparently sharing one piece of their own AI-assisted work — process, checks, and open questions included — and ask teams to run AI through a full gather-brainstorm-draft-critique cycle instead of skipping straight to a polished draft.
