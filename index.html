Specialized Kids Aren't Resilient Kids

Specialized kids are not resilient kids. It's pretty simple why: if you can only do one thing, if you're built for only one type of environment, you have trouble when conditions change. Our job as parents is to raise resilient kids with a broad set of skills and interests — who don't limit what they can do and can't be put into a box. But it's also our job as parents to be that kind of person, too.

We have to be resilient, flexible, well-rounded human beings not just to survive the stresses of parenting, but if we want to raise kids for the real world. In his book Of Boys and Men, Richard Reeves writes about how fragile our attitude toward gender often is: "The role of mothers has been expanded to include breadwinning as well as caring, but the role of fathers has not been expanded to include caring as well as breadwinning."

Just as we want kids who can be "acceptable at a dance and invaluable in a shipwreck," we as parents should strive to embody that same versatility. We should be more than just providers or caretakers — we should be explorers, creators, learners, and models of resilience ourselves. Parenting isn't about slipping into a specialized role; it's about showing our children that it's possible to grow in multiple directions at once, embracing both strength and sensitivity, ambition and humility, stability and adaptability.

So let's be that example. Let's contain multitudes — showing our kids that they do, too.

High Demand for Hybrids Leaves US Automakers in Park

  • In the US, Kia offers seven hybrid models, Hyundai six, Honda five, and Toyota 18. Ford and GM offer two combined.
