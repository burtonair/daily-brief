Judge Won’t Force Google to Sell Off Its Ad Business

US District Judge Leonie Brinkema ruled Google won’t have to divest its ad exchange despite finding last year that its ad server acted as an illegal monopoly — the latest sign that courts are wary of breaking up Big Tech, and a bad omen for upcoming antitrust trials against Amazon and Apple. Morning Brew

The ruling. In an abbreviated opinion, Brinkema rejected the DOJ’s request to splinter Google’s ad business, though she did accept other proposals to curb the company’s control over how publishers use its ad tech. Her full ruling, once confidential information is redacted, won’t be available for two weeks.

ICYMI. The DOJ and 17 states sued Google three years ago, alleging its ad server — which publishers use to run ads, and which the DOJ said held 91% of global market share — was illegally tied to its ad exchange, the marketplace used to buy and sell ads. Last year, Brinkema ruled in the DOJ’s favor on the merits, but yesterday she refused to order the exchange’s sale.

A pattern with the courts. This marks the second time this year Google has beaten back a monopoly case without losing a business arm: a district judge previously found Google illegally dominated online search but let it keep Chrome, reasoning AI would probably disrupt search anyway. Meta similarly avoided a breakup in its own social-networking monopoly case once TikTok’s rise complicated the picture.

Why it matters. The recent run of Big Tech punishments with no teeth doesn’t bode well for the government’s upcoming antitrust trials against Amazon and Apple.

You’re Going to Need Slack (Do You Have It?) Daily Dad

The thing about having kids is that extra and unexpected stuff is always happening. Somebody gets sick. Somebody’s having a hard time. Somebody needs to be driven across town. Somebody needs expensive orthodontics or qualified for a really cool after-school program. Somebody—your teenager who doesn’t normally reach out—is suddenly open to spending time with you.

The question, what defines us as a parent, is how available we are to deal with all this. Emotionally yes, but also logistically. Do you have the sick and vacation days to be able to stay home with them? Do you have the energy to shuttle them to the place they need to go? Do you have the funds to pay for that expense? Do you have the freedom (the power) to change plans and reschedule that meeting until next week?

We learned during the pandemic just how vulnerable “real-time” and “just-in-time” supply chains were — they simply couldn’t absorb sudden and unexpected events. So it goes with parenting. If you’re behind, if you’re stretched too thin, if you haven’t prepared for a moment like this, you’re not going to be able to meet it. We need redundancies. We need backups. We need flexibility. We need slack.

Or rather, our kids are going to need that from us. Will we have it? Well, now is the time to answer that question—now is the time to get it so we (they) have it when it’s needed.

Report: OpenAI’s Astra Uses a Technique That Clouds AI Safety Monitoring

OpenAI’s upcoming Astra model reportedly uses “recurrent depth” — looping over the same text repeatedly before answering — which boosts coding and computer-use skills but produces reasoning that’s harder to read, according to The Information. The Rundown AI

The technique has the model analyze the same text in repeated loops before answering, squeezing extra intelligence out without making the model larger — but the loops reportedly produce thinking outputs that are pure math instead of readable reasoning.

OpenAI reportedly dialed the loops back so Astra still writes out its reasoning, and its blog promises extra monitoring of that reasoning at launch. Chief Scientist Jakub Pachocki says he wants to “prevent a race into unmonitorability” from “confused reporting,” while calling monitoring “fragile.”

Why it matters: The alarm here is less about Astra, which OpenAI says still shows its work, and more about a performance race that tempts the industry to loop harder and monitor less — Pachocki himself admits current monitoring is already fraying for reasons unrelated to Astra.
